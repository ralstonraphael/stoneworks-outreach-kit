# 01. Who we write to (ICP)

Stoneworks sells AI implementation work to land services firms and energy operators. This page is for everyone who sends. It explains what kind of client we are looking for, why, who we leave alone, and what we do and do not say. It works the same for any name you pick up. Industry terms are explained in the glossary at the bottom.

## The client we want

- **Companies:** independent land services and brokerage firms (title, abstracting, leasing, right-of-way or ROW, curative, due diligence, contract landmen). Second, small private E&P land departments and minerals shops. Small operators and midstream companies come after that.
- **People:** the person who feels the hours directly and can say yes to a conversation. That is an owner or principal; a VP, Director or Manager of Land; a COO or CEO at a small firm; an operations VP; or whoever runs business systems. After a first call, also the person who does the work day to day, such as whoever runs billing.
- **Size:** no fixed minimum or maximum. Smaller firms are usually the better fit, from a handful of people up to a few hundred, because the decision maker is reachable and can move fast. Judge by the work, not the headcount. A pilot needs a task that takes 5 or more hours a week, and a small shop can have one.
- **Place:** the US. Check the time zone before you schedule.

## Why they fit

This is the thinking behind every qualification below. If a lead has most of these, write. If it has few, skip.

1. **The work repeats.** Land and energy back offices run on recurring paperwork: timesheets, invoices, status reports, intake, filings. Repeating work is where AI helps first.
2. **The inputs already exist.** The documents and data are on hand, so there is something to work with on day one.
3. **A person can check the output.** We are not asking anyone to trust a machine with a title opinion. A person reviews, so a mistake is caught before it matters.
4. **The owner feels the cost.** In a small firm, an hour of back-office time is a real hour of someone senior or someone billable. That makes a saving easy to see.
5. **The crowded space is not ours.** Vendors already sell title and runsheet tools. We care about the firm-specific work around those tools, where little is on offer.
6. **They can decide.** We want a person who can say yes to a call and later to a small first project, without a long procurement process.

## Signals that make a lead stronger

- Uses many contract landmen, field staff or outside vendors.
- Hiring for back-office, regulatory, land administration or analyst roles, which suggests real office volume.
- Serves several operators or clients at once.
- Runs multiple regions or project types.
- The person has posted, spoken or been quoted about their work, or shares a real reference point with the sender (same event, school or former employer).
- Already uses some AI or software for people and projects. That is a good sign, because they are open to the idea.

## Signals that make a lead weaker

- Very large or public companies, where decisions go through procurement and IT.
- A firm that is in the middle of a sale or merger.
- A single person with no staff and no recurring paperwork.
- A role with no say over how work gets done, with no way to reach someone who does.
- A site or profile that is out of date, so you cannot find one true fact to write about.

## Who we skip, hold or never contact

- **Skip:** law firms, universities, associations, banks, software vendors, the major oil companies, large public operators, competitors (firms that sell AI or systems consulting to the same buyers), and companies outside the US unless Ralston clears them. Independent landmen on personal email addresses are not buyers, so skip them too.
- **Hold:** one person per company first. A second name at the same firm waits until the first replies, or until the day 17 email has gone out with no reply. Also hold anyone whose title conflicts between sources until you have checked it.
- **Already taken:** any company on the Claimed or Team Claims tab of `Claimed_Companies.xlsx`. That includes every company from the AAPL 72nd Annual Meeting attendee list (Ralston's) and Andrew's list. Run `tools/check_claimed.py` before you research anyone.
- **Never contact:** anyone who has asked us to stop, and any company with a sale or acquisition pending or just closed. The Do Not Contact tab of `Claimed_Companies.xlsx` is the source of truth. Before every send, search the company name with "acquisition" and "sold", in case something new has happened.

## How we qualify a lead

Ask these questions in order. The first no ends it.

1. **Is it the right kind of company?** Land services, a small operator's land team, or a minerals shop. If not, skip.
2. **Is it allowed?** Not already claimed, not a competitor, not public, not outside the US, not on Do Not Contact, no sale pending. If not, skip.
3. **Is it the right person?** Their title is confirmed on their site or LinkedIn, and they have a say. If not, fix the contact or find the right one.
4. **Does the work repeat?** You can picture timesheets, invoices, reports or intake in their week. If you cannot, pick another lead.
5. **Is there a reason to write now?** One true, specific thing about them, with a page you can point to. If you have none after five minutes, pick another lead.
6. **Can you write it honestly?** You have a true reason for writing, yours or your team's, worded so it never implies you did the work (the ladder in 02_Email_Playbook).

When you have more leads than sends, send first to the ones with more signals from the list above, a role that clearly owns the work, and a fresh reason to write.

## What we say, and what we do not

**We say:**

- We help land and energy teams find what AI could take off their plate, and we are honest about what it can't.
- The call is free, and so is the read afterwards. We are starting with a small number of conversations.
- Everyone we talk with gets the November rundown (the offer in 02_Email_Playbook).
- Questions about their week: how the work gets done, who does it, where the hours go.
- A small first step is always possible, and they see the scope and the cost in writing before deciding anything.

**We do not say:**

- Anything about price in a first email or a first call, not even a range.
- A promise of results, hours saved or money saved.
- That we have built things for land or energy companies. We have not. The documented track record is construction.
- Anything that implies the sender personally did work that a teammate did.
- A diagnosis of their problems ("your invoicing is a mess") or a guess at their use case in the first email.
- Flattery. State a fact instead.
- A claim that we connect to a named vendor's software.
- Title or runsheet automation. Vendors already sell it, and title errors carry liability. If it comes up, say a person would always review the work, and do not promise it.
- Anything about a competitor, or another prospect, by name.

## Research a lead in five minutes

1. **Website.** What they do, who they serve, the team page. Could repeating paperwork exist here?
2. **LinkedIn.** The person's current title and tenure, and the size band. Verify a title if sources disagree.
3. **Job posts.** A back-office or regulatory opening suggests real office volume.
4. **News.** Search the name with "acquisition," "sale" and "sold." Anything pending goes to the hold rules.
5. **Choose one thing** and keep its source page with the draft.

Mark each fact confirmed (their site, a job post, their own words), aggregator only (ZoomInfo, RocketReach, revenue estimates), or unknown. Never put an aggregator number in an email.

**The one true thing.** Could this sentence go to anyone else in their industry? If yes, it is not the one. Strongest first: something they did or said (a talk, post, hire, award); something true of their position that they would agree with, such as working for many operators at once; a shared reference point (same event, school, former employer); last, a company fact anyone could look up. Describe the category of work and do not guess their problem. Two people at one firm get different lines. With nothing after five minutes, pick another lead.

## Is this lead a good fit?

1. A land services firm, a small operator's land team, or a minerals shop, under about 500 people.
2. Not a competitor, not a public company, not outside the US, and not an independent on a personal address.
3. No sale pending, no assets sold. You checked the news today.
4. The person fits the role list, and the title is confirmed on their site or LinkedIn.
5. The company is FREE on `tools/check_claimed.py`, and nobody else at the firm has been written to, by you or a teammate.
6. The work plausibly repeats and a person can review the output.
7. You found one true specific thing, with a source, and a true reason for writing: yours, or your team's, worded so it never implies you did the work (the ladder in 02_Email_Playbook).

A "no" on 1 to 3 means skip. A "no" on 4 to 7 means fix it, then send.

## Glossary

- **AAPL:** the American Association of Professional Landmen, the main industry association.
- **Landman:** a person who researches who owns land and mineral rights and negotiates leases and access for energy companies.
- **CPL, RPL, RL:** landman credentials (Certified Professional Landman, Registered Professional Landman, Registered Landman).
- **E&P:** exploration and production, the companies that drill and run wells. An **operator** runs the wells.
- **Midstream:** pipelines and the gathering and processing between the well and the market.
- **Minerals shop:** a firm that owns, buys or manages mineral interests.
- **ROW:** right-of-way, the access land that pipelines, power lines and roads need.
- **Title, abstracting, runsheet, curative:** title work establishes who owns a tract. Abstracting summarizes the documents in its chain of ownership. A runsheet lists those documents in order. Curative fixes problems found in the title.
- **Tract:** a defined parcel of land.
- **A&D:** acquisition and divestiture, the buying and selling of oil and gas assets, which involves diligence on the leases.
- **Lease abstraction:** pulling the key terms out of leases into a summary.
