# Research basis

Reviewed September 18, 2026.

This note records the evidence used to shape the skill. It is maintenance context, not a detector checklist. No single phrase proves that text came from an AI system.

## Findings that changed the skill

- Repetition and density matter more than isolated words. Community discussions repeatedly distinguish occasional legitimate use from formulaic reuse across paragraphs.
- Readers object to empty rhetoric as much as punctuation. Common complaints include fake profundity, restated conclusions, generic transitions, forced metaphors, promotional tone, and a shared voice across unrelated writers.
- Formatting can create the same effect. Excessive headings, bold-first bullets, emoji markers, rigid lists, and small unnecessary tables make ordinary content feel templated.
- Voice loss is a central complaint. Writers report that polished output can become less recognizable as their own because specific phrasing is replaced by generic, high-probability prose.
- Sycophancy is a separate quality problem. Agreement and praise should follow evidence, not precede it automatically.
- Word blacklists are weak. Research finds measurable shifts in vocabulary, but words change across model generations and many flagged words remain valid English.
- Deliberate mistakes are not a sound remedy. Strong editing restores specificity and intent instead of adding typos or gaming detectors.
- Plain-language practices remain useful: write for the actual audience, prefer active voice and direct verbs, keep sections short, and remove nominalizations and avoidable words.

## Sources

### Descriptive field guide

Wikipedia contributors, "Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing"

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing

Useful for recurring content, language, grammar, and formatting patterns. The page explicitly warns that the signs are descriptive, context-dependent, and insufficient as proof of AI authorship.

### Lexical research

Dmitry Kobak, Rita González-Márquez, Emőke-Ágnes Horvát, and Jan Lause, "Delving into ChatGPT usage in academic writing through excess vocabulary," arXiv:2406.07016v2, July 3, 2024.

https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v2

The study analyzes 14.4 million PubMed abstracts and reports a sharp post-2022 rise in style words associated with LLM-assisted writing. It supports treating word choice as a density signal, not banning every occurrence.

### Sycophancy research

Mrinank Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models," arXiv:2310.13548v4, May 10, 2025.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548

The study finds sycophantic behavior across several assistants and links part of it to preference feedback that rewards agreement with a user's views.

### Community reports

Reddit, r/WritingWithAI, "What are the 'AI-isms' that always give away bad AI-generated writing in a reddit post?"

https://www.reddit.com/r/WritingWithAI/comments/1mkwxnl/what_are_the_aiisms_that_always_give_away_bad/

Recurring complaints include overused em dashes, negative contrast, rhetorical questions, bullet and emphasis clutter, canned preambles, repeated transitions, strange metaphors, rigid three-part phrasing, and prose that says little at length. Several commenters stress that frequency and misuse are the problem, not ordinary punctuation or vocabulary by itself.

Hacker News discussion, "LLM Writing Tropes.md"

https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47291513

The discussion emphasizes reader time, loss of individual voice, generic corporate tone, excessive tokens for little value, and the difference between editing real ideas and laundering empty output.

Tropes.fyi, "AI Writing Tropes to Avoid"

https://tropes.fyi/tropes-md

This community-maintained catalog supplied useful labels for newer patterns such as premise stacking, reasoning narration, synonym cycling, repeated summaries, canned suspense, and unnecessary rhetorical framing. The skill uses its categories as leads, then applies independent editorial judgment.

### Reporting on reader and writer response

Emma Madden, "More Typos, Fewer Em Dashes: Writers Are Creating an Anti-AI 'Literary Counterculture'," WIRED, July 29, 2026.

https://www.wired.com/story/more-typos-fewer-em-dashes-writers-are-creating-an-anti-ai-literary-counterculture/

The article reports growing avoidance of formulaic patterns and stronger interest in first-person specificity and idiosyncratic voice. It also captures the risk of overcorrecting through deliberate mistakes. The skill rejects that tactic.

Ibrahim Diallo, "Why we hate AI-assisted articles," March 2, 2026.

https://idiallo.com/blog/why-we-hate-llm-articles

This first-person account focuses on bloated rewrites, borrowed quips, uniform voice, and the loss of recognizable authorship after AI-assisted editing.

### Plain-language baseline

U.S. General Services Administration, Digital.gov, "Writing for understanding."

https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing

The guide supports audience-specific language, active voice, present tense, direct verbs, short sections, and removal of hidden verbs and avoidable complexity.

## Maintenance cautions

- Refresh examples periodically. Model habits and public reactions change.
- Keep user intent above trend avoidance.
- Do not turn the skill into an authorship detector.
- Do not replace one recognizable template with another.
- Keep hard rules few. Use contextual judgment for the larger watchlist.
